## Beating vs refining
- Beating = bars working against a bedplate, batch (Hollander beater, still used for jute, hemp, flax, cotton); refining = continuous conical or disk refiners, fibers move parallel to bar crossings. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.25]
- Hollander beater: consistency 4-8%, roll surface speed about 700 m/min, roll load zero to 12 bar. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.26]
- Mechanisms: shear (rolling, twisting, tension) and normal stresses (bending, crushing); Fig 13-7 five steps from wad gathering to dispersion. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]

## Effects on fibers (Table 13-1)
- Primary: primary wall removed (fines), bruising and swelling, hydration, flexibility, external fibrillation and foliation, shortening. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]
- Secondary: wall cracks, stretching or compression, hemicellulose "gels", straightening at low consistency and curling at high. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]
- Internal fibrillation is "generally seen as the most important effect of refining". [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]
- Cutting is usually undesirable (slower drainage, lower strength) but sometimes wanted for formation or drainage control. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]
- Drainability falls quickly during refining, mainly because fines increase. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]

## Energy and intensity
- Net energy = measured brake (gross) energy minus no-load (backed-off) energy; no-load energy depends on refiner type. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.29]
- Specific edge load = net power divided by total bar-edge length contacting stock per unit time (see [[specific-edge-load]]). [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]
- Specific Surface Load theory: edge-to-edge (cutting), edge-to-surface (brushing), surface-to-surface (fibrillation). [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.27]
- Low-intensity refining gives more fibrillation, less cutting; gradual energy input beats an abrupt dose; mill refiners cannot copy lab beaters and need many refiners for low intensity. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.29]

## Variables (Table 13-2) and pulp response
- Raw material, equipment (bar size, groove depth, dams, bar angle, peripheral speed) and process (temperature, pH, consistency, production rate, applied energy). [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.29]
- Kraft harder to refine than sulphite; soda easiest; unbleached harder than bleached; high-lignin pulps swell less. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.29]
- High-yield mechanical and chemimechanical pulps are generally not refined in the mill (severe cutting). [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.29]
- Hemicellulose-rich pulps refine easily; dissolving pulps high in alpha-cellulose refine slowly. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.29]
- Dried chemical pulps, including recycled fibers, are harder to refine than never-dried. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.30]
- pH above 7 speeds beating; higher consistency gives more fiber-to-fiber contact and less cutting. [DOC d9815fa5-40a2-474b-9932-eaf665991fc0 p.30]

## Effect on paper
- Long-fiber pulps lose strength as fibers are cut; burst, tensile, folding endurance rise from better bonding; slower drainage, denser sheet, lower porosity and opacity, poorer dimensional stability. [DOC 01a335fe-bfba-4f11-b32c-df2de03d8521 p.5]
- Fig 13-23: tensile and burst rise as freeness falls (about 800 to 500 CSF); tear rises first, peaks near 800 CSF, then falls. [DOC 01a335fe-bfba-4f11-b32c-df2de03d8521 p.5]
- Paired properties (tear vs tensile or burst) are more useful than single-property plots; gentle vs harsh beating gives two curves of papermaking potential (Fig 13-25). [DOC 01a335fe-bfba-4f11-b32c-df2de03d8521 p.5]
