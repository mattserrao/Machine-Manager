- Fibres must be conformable and bond at contact points; conformability shows as sheet formation, bonding as tensile or burst [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.18]
- Table 1-4 length (mm) / diameter (um): softwood 4.0 / 40; hardwood 2.0 / 22; bagasse 1.7 / 20; cotton 30 / 20; bamboo 2.8 / 15 [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.19]
- Table 1-5 DP: native cellulose 3500; cotton linters 1000-3000; commercial wood pulps 600-1500; rayon 200-600 [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.19]
- Best papermaking balance: most lignin removed, substantial hemicellulose kept; beating removes primary walls so fibres hydrate and swell [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.21]
- Hydrogen bonds form between hydroxyl groups as water is evaporated; paper strength depends more on inter-fibre bonds than fibre strength [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.22]
- Fig 1-10 softwood chemical pulp beating: tear peaks near 15 min (~220) then falls to ~140 by 75 min; freeness falls [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.22]
- Extractives usually under 1% for many North American species; southern pines higher [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.21]
- Softwoods: tracheids; hardwoods: libriform fibres plus vessels; Table 2-2 spruce 99% fibres by weight vs birch 86% fibres, 9% vessels [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.25, p.29]
- Cell wall: S2 layer holds 80-90% of fibre wall; fibril angle helix to the fibre axis [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.26, p.27]
- Higher wood density: slower beating, lower tensile/burst/fold, greater bulk, higher tear [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.28]
- Table 2-3 (length mm / density lb per cu ft): longleaf pine 4.9 / 41; loblolly 3.6 / 36; black spruce 3.5 / 30; balsam fir 3.5 / 25; Douglas fir 3.9 / 34; redwood 6.1 / 25; aspen 1.04 / 27; birch 1.85 / 38; beech 1.20 / 45 [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.30]
- Compression wood forms on the underside of leaning softwoods [DOC e80d6cb4-2ca6-4bbd-8748-ea1f68624dad p.30]

## From part 2 (book pp. ~17-36) [[doc-smook-fundamentals-part-02]]
- Compression wood fibres: rounded, thick-walled, large fibril angle, respond poorly to beating or refining; tension wood (upper side of leaning stems) gives higher chemical pulp yield but fibres are hard to beat and bond poorly [DOC cb6a70a2-a00a-4244-809b-d0effdcde3f1 p.1]
- Bark is generally a contaminant; extractives consume chemical and partly pulped bark remains as dirt [DOC cb6a70a2-a00a-4244-809b-d0effdcde3f1 p.1]
- Fibre length and wall thickness are the two key characteristics; tear is roughly proportional to fibre length; thin-walled fibres collapse into ribbons and bond better, thick-walled fibres give bulky, open, high-tear, low-burst sheet [DOC cb6a70a2-a00a-4244-809b-d0effdcde3f1 p.1]
- Coarseness = weight of fibre wall per specified length, mg per 100 m; L/T ratio indexes flexibility [DOC cb6a70a2-a00a-4244-809b-d0effdcde3f1 p.3]
- Table 2-4 (length mm / L:T / coarseness mg per 100 m): birch 1.8 / 500 / 5-8; red gum 1.7 / 300 / 8-10; black spruce 3.5 / 700 / 14-19; red cedar 3.5 / 1400 / 15-17; southern pine 4.6 / 700 / 20-30; Douglas-fir 3.9 / 700 / 25-32; redwood 6.1 / 1700 / 25-35 [DOC cb6a70a2-a00a-4244-809b-d0effdcde3f1 p.3]
- Species is identified by pit shape and orientation (TAPPI T263, T401; Strelis and Kennedy) [DOC cb6a70a2-a00a-4244-809b-d0effdcde3f1 p.4]
